Almost a year ago I wrote my [last post](/blog/ai-is-scary) about my thoughts, feelings and expectations towards AI as part of the software lifecycle. Eleven months later and I feel comfortable in saying that for myself those predictions have come true. Plus, while not entirely without consequences, broadly the changes this has had on my work have been quite… positive? Let's talk about that.

First, start with the facts. AI tools did get drastically better again since then, their output being more than acceptable in almost all cases. Especially when paired with good instructions, guardrails, and reviews. The code generated by modern models is hard to distinguish from what I would consider good contributions by a human. Not all of it of course, but once again, broadly speaking. All the while they are executing at speeds that cannot be matched by a human engineer, or not me at the very least. Aside from the occasional line here or there, I personally haven't had the need to write code manually in months. My whole focus has shifted towards planning and executing projects, and designing the wider systems surrounding them. The change in workflow is, as far as I understand, a change that has been happening in most of the industry. Definitely faster for some than others, but unmistakeably steady.

**From algorithms to experiences**

The opinions on what such a shift meant in engineering workflows are also certainly varied, to put it generously. I do think we, as in the industry as a whole, are still in the process of finding the right balance. Balance which is hard to find as it is gets shaken up by each new model release again, but I think the major leaps have been slower to come recently. I do understand the people that are frustrated. It's a tough time, especially if the act of coding itself is the part that brings you the most joy in your role (or spare time). Programming had been one of my favourite hobbies for practically as long as I had access to a computer. I loved getting lost in the tiny details and the ability it gave me to shape and design everything exactly as I wanted to. I used to love sitting down for hours on end and just hack away at my code. Or, at least so I thought. In the last few months I had a realisation about my relationship with creating software, I don't think it ever was the act of writing the code that I loved. Sure, it was undoubtedly part of the whole, but at the core of it and what really scratches the itch for me is creating a piece of work that I am proud of. Be it a website or app that I had to think about and refine to my liking. Something where I had to find solutions that make everything click together just right, pieces that fit each other so perfectly that they fulfill my vision of what I wanted to create exactly. Ultimately, giving me the result I wanted, not even really for anyone else but myself. All of that was very hard work, especially refining after thinking everything was locked in, just to find that one extra rough corner to smooth over. Instead, AI enables me to do exactly this in a heartbeat. I can orchestrate multiple helpers and iterate on the pieces, edge cases and details so much faster than I was ever able to before. And that brings me immense joy.

At first I didn't think that letting go of writing the code manually would work for me, but as time went on I realised how much more time and enjoyment I got out of focusing on the experience, perhaps the infrastructure or performance instead. Now, don't mistake such an approach for mindless "vibe coding" or token slop. Assuming that such a transition was imminent, I made a very concerted effort to actually shape the way I think about AI checks and guardrails, to enable this shift in how I approach development. I still believe that the last step of every change going into a well rounded, functioning system should be a human review, at least for the time being. There is a level of care in being precise with a change that a machine still is not able to accomplish and as such does not understand. For the work I do at SimplyDo I pushed adoption of a rigorous, but targeted testing infrastructure. We invested heavily in a test suite that functions more like genuine activity and users interacting with our application, instead of allowing the AI to freely spam 100s of unit tests. On the surface, working with an agentic workflow is not that different to working with any other colleague. They might have a different approach, or a different style in tackling a problem, the important part is finding that agreeable middleground. The main difference being that another coworker will have a genuine reason and opinions for their style, or might push back on your own. AI does not really do this, it is rarely able to explain when it makes a decision that I consider wrong and more often than not reverts back to its sycophantic nature.

That inability of framing its own viewpoint outside of what it considers "objectively" correct or incorrect is what I consider a key detail in what it takes to find the enjoyment that I have been speaking about. Even when I don't write a lot of code by hand anymore, it is very important to me that I still take pride in what is _my_ work. I am aware that the individual artefacts might not be as perfect as if I had spent hours and hours pouring over every single detail, every single call signature, every single line placed by hand. However, that doesn't mean I stopped caring. If anything, I care more than ever. I care about the experiences I craft now more than I ever did before. I can iterate over seemingly countless designs and decisions in a way that would have been unthinkable before. Personally, I believe that my outputs, the part that someone else actually interacts with and uses, have been of so much higher quality than before. I now have time to obsess over those details exclusively, instead of having to worry about whether I built my latest abstraction just right. As is often the case, the truth is likely somewhere inbetween here. For the record: compared to some voices, I do think code does still matter. I believe that as a software engineer you do need to know how the data flows through your application and you do need to make sure that it is protected, performant, etc. It just doesn't really matter that it is yourself that writes it and I certainly _feel_ better being able to focus my whole energy on results, rather than those implementation details.

**The human touch, forever lost?**

One video that spiralled me into deep thought recently was this really quite interesting piece on art and, specifically, what constitutes "worthwhile" art. The author of the video, at times humourosly, but generally quite insightfully dismantles the idea that a painting has to be complex or be difficult to create to be impactful or important. Further, it addresses a common sticking point that even if you could be technically skilled enough to paint it, the fact is that you did not. You may wonder how a video about abstract paintings is at all related to the topic at hand. The reason why this struck such a chord with me is a parallel I personally see in regards to what people now consider losing the "art of programming". First off, I do believe everyone is entitled to have their own perspective on this, as it affects everyone differently. What I have come to understand myself though is the following: Having the ability to create code more quickly, doesn't lessen what I create with it. At least not in the way that you might think it does. I still always have a vision, and I still have this almost obsessive approach to designing software. Whether or not you consider this "art" I'll leave to yourself, the point I'm making is that the result is more than a mere sum of its parts. I still arrange all of the elements, I just do it in a different process where I delegate, instruct and oversee. Obviously it's not the same as putting a brush on a canvas, or composing a piece of music, but it's also very far from lacking creative input altogether aka "hey robot, create thing, make no mistakes". I think this is where a large portion of the divergence surrounding how people feel about the use of AI in software comes from. Is there people that are doing exactly this? Certainly. As you can tell by the hundreds, thousands of pieces of lifeless software being created every day. This however, isn't me, and I wish that never to be me.

Whether you watch it or not, my argument effectively boils down to: You could not have "prompted this". Could you have given an AI the instructions to look at my work and make it recreate a result that looks like my work? Sure, and it would probably do a good job. In that situation, you (and the AI) would lack the understanding of every decision for every interaction I made along the way. Every piece that I planned and arranged to make sure that the end result looks, feels, and works the way I wanted it to. You will be missing the _reason_ why things work (or don't work) the way they do and why I might have chosen that direction intentionally. Likely this means, not only is it going to be much less useful to you or anyone else without that context (or investing time into understand that context), but also who will instill future direction. Where do you go if your input was just being able to copy something someone else had already created. I'm not talking about cloning to improve aspects of an existing system, that's admittedly a more open-ended discussion, but simply creating for creations sake.

I put a major distinction between using AI as a tool to shape what you'd like to see exist, versus using AI to generate (or copy) without much thought at all.

**Intentional or mindless, good or bad, progress or downfall**

Practically every engineer I know complaining about the issues with such generated content has reflected on it with a similar response: "I don't dislike or hate AI in principle, but I do hate when I am confronted with unfiltered, unchecked AI output". At this point, most of us will have been presented with a borderline incomprehensible wall of text generated by Claude et al. The prevalent animosity towards AI output of others, compared to AI output of yourself ties in with my point above. Really, AI can be fantastic at breaking down changes and explaining them. Just as it can be wonderfully helpful in your own creative processes, but only if (and it's a big if) the model is guided correctly and it's outputs are handled accordingly. Realistically, noone else other than yourself should see most of the content your AI generated directly. It already needs to be explicitly told what to prioritise and highlight for your own understanding as a human reader. To someone else, you should take the explaining part upon yourself. Think about it this way, if you cannot explain something, you likely won't have been intentional enough when creating it either.

Having "taste" is a major buzzword in online discourse about AI, ad absurdum in all honesty. Regardless of what it is called though, I do believe the one differentiating factor between "good" and "bad" AI use is relatively straightforward. It's investing that extra energy to apply your own human intuition and discretion, instead of accepting the bare tokens an AI wants to (and will) generate. Unfortunately, humans are lazy by default. Where in other cases being lazy usually just creates an issue for yourself, lazily generating masses of text that only offer vague descriptions just makes life difficult for everyone else. This juxtaposition in care has brought drastic changes to how code is produced, distributed and looked after. As it was custom, there is a lot of people that are trying to back their skills by boasting a variety of contributions, but with the rise of AI it's much easier to just point an agent at a repo and make it generate unchecked PRs, instead of providing intentional additons. A lot of open source communities are shutting down or changing their approach to third-party contributions because of the prevalence and volume of AI written changes. Required AI-use labels or statements have become pretty standard. Some only accept contribution based on accepted issues.

There has also been a constant influx of security incidents discovered by automated processes, almost daily there is news of breaches or must-patch vulnerabilities in one or multiple of the largest languages and frameworks (e.g. just the tip of iceberg <Ref text="PHP" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP2Shell" />, <Ref text="React" href="https://react2shell.com/" />, <Ref text="Ruby" href="https://watchtowr.com/intelligence/ruby-cve-2026-132825-faq/" />). While that is scary since it appears that everything is falling apart at the seams, I choose to believe it's more that the technology is catching up with what we have always known and patching the gaps that were long overdue to be sealed. Everything is currently evolving rapidly and there undoubtedly are teething issues with this new omnipresent technology, but I remain optimistic. We are headed into (or are already arriving in) a future where much reptetitive work is going away. Effectively meaning we have to channel our energy elsewhere, and as part of that it's going to matter to think about what or where that is, instead of just thinking less about the work we have always been doing.

Compared to the start of the year, to me it's now less certain as to where we will stand in another 12 months this time. I hope that once the dust settles on the ability to generate anything at a whim, there is going to be a resurgence on expressiveness and a realisation that having a real connection to what you do, and showing an effort to care will still matter, more than ever perhaps. All I can do now is embrace this change, build my skills, continue to learn, apply it and interact with it. Being empathetic to others in this process, trying to understand how they are affected, and what the path forward is to mark this an overall positive bit of progress in our lifetime.
